# A genomic language model writes working genes from context alone

*Brian Hie's group used Evo to generate anti-CRISPR proteins and toxin-antitoxin systems that function in bacteria, including genes with no detectable similarity to any natural protein.*

**What was done.** Aditi Merchant, Samuel King, Eric Nguyen and Brian Hie at the Arc Institute and Stanford University published this peer reviewed study in Nature on 19 November 2025. Hie is the corresponding author. Their idea, called semantic design, uses Evo, a genomic language model, as an autocomplete: a DNA prompt that supplies the genomic context around a function of interest steers the model to generate new sequences enriched for that function. Because Evo has learned how genes sit together across prokaryotic genomes, the context itself specifies what to make, without structural templates, known conservation or task-specific fine-tuning.

**What they made and measured.** For anti-CRISPRs, the team prompted with known Cas9-targeting operons, filtered the generations and screened 84 candidates in E. coli, where functional proteins preserve antibiotic resistance by blocking CRISPR cleavage. About 17 percent were active, and five strong proteins, EvoAcr1 to EvoAcr5, protected against SpCas9 in both survival and phage infection assays, with limited sequence identity to known anti-CRISPRs. For toxin-antitoxin systems, 10 generated type II toxin candidates were tested and two were active; the strongest, EvoRelE1, then served as a prompt to generate cognate antitoxins, and EvoAT1 to EvoAT4 rescued growth. A type III antitoxin, EvoAT6, rescued growth against the ToxN toxin, confirming a generated non-coding RNA. Some validated genes had no significant similarity to natural proteins.

**What it shows.** Earlier generative genomics could produce plausible sequence but struggled to aim it at a specified function. Here the function is articulated through genomic context rather than a label, and it yields working proteins and a working regulatory RNA at useful success rates. Extending this to millions of prompts produced SynGenome, a database of over 120 billion base pairs of generated sequence, which is the scale that marks the boundary.

**Caveats.** Success rates are reported per campaign and rest on tens of tested constructs, and several candidates could not be cloned. All functional tests were in bacteria, and selection relied in part on structure prediction rather than solved structures.

**Why it matters for provenance.** When a model can write functional genes that resemble nothing in the databases, sequence similarity alone no longer traces origin, and a database of billions of generated base pairs makes a durable record of machine-written DNA a practical necessity.

Source: Aditi T. Merchant, Samuel H. King, Eric Nguyen and Brian L. Hie. Semantic design of functional de novo genes from a genomic language model. Nature, published 19 November 2025, doi 10.1038/s41586-025-09749-7. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09749-7
