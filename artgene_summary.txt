ArtGene-Archive: Project Summary

As LLMs begin producing novel proteins and genes at scale, we need a way to
tell AI-designed sequences apart from those already catalogued as synthetic.
The distinction matters because the two carry different risks and different
provenance questions.

The synthetic divisions at ENA and GenBank record things like plasmids,
cloning vectors, and codon-optimised genes: human-assembled, but built from
known biological parts. AI-designed sequences are different. They may have no
natural relative, are produced by a specific model under specific conditions,
and can't be traced or safety-checked against any existing organism.

So what if every AI-designed gene carried an invisible signature, baked
straight into its DNA? That's what I'm building with TINSEL, a watermark
algorithm. Because the same protein can be spelled with different codons,
TINSEL hides a provenance tag in those synonymous choices. The protein stays
exactly the same, but the DNA now records who made it, and the mark survives
re-synthesis, so even a sequence found in the wild, in wastewater, or anywhere
else can be traced back.

The goal is that no sequencing machine should synthesize an AI-generated
sequence until a certificate confirms its provenance is already on record. On
top of that, every AI-generated sequence should pass biosafety screening for
structure, toxicity, and ecological risk before it's certified. That's what
I'm working on.

But can we prove the watermark idea actually works? We can test it
experimentally: express a watermarked GFP reporter protein alongside an
unmarked control, and check that both show equal expression and fluorescence.
If they match, the watermark is biologically invisible.
